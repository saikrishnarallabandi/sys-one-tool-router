\title{Post-Training Typed-Decision Models with Proper-Scoring Rewards:\\Accuracy, Calibration, and the Underconfidence Effect}
\author{Sai Krishna Rallabandi}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Calibrated decisions require probabilities that mean what they say, yet the
post-training recipes that maximise decision accuracy routinely damage
calibration, and those that preserve it leave accuracy on the table. We show
both effects can be had in sequence: post-training a 4B prefill-only
decision model with soft-target cross-entropy plus Brier and
ranked-probability-score terms raises held-out decision accuracy 0.665 to
0.800 and cuts KL from gold threefold, while expected calibration error
regresses 0.091 to 0.162. A reliability analysis traces the regression to
systematic \emph{underconfidence} induced by distributional supervision, and
a single temperature (T*~=~0.35, fit on disjoint training data) restores ECE
to 0.031 with accuracy unchanged. The result is a small, single-pass model
that beats an accuracy-first specialist and a calibration-first baseline on
both axes at once.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Autonomous systems increasingly act on model outputs without a human in the
loop: routing tickets, triaging incidents, approving invoices. Each such act
is a \emph{typed decision} — a choice among named options, a score on an
ordered scale, or a yes/no verdict — reported as a probability distribution.
The distribution is the product: downstream thresholds (automate, defer,
escalate) are only meaningful if 0.80 means right 80\% of the time. The
problem is important because miscalibrated automation either acts when it
should defer or defers when it could act, and both failures compound at
machine scale.

The problem is hard because the standard post-training objectives pull in
opposite directions. Preference-based RL (RLHF) rewards text that
\emph{sounds} right and narrows output distributions, the wrong objective
for unattended decisions. Verifiable-reward RL (RLVR) maximises accuracy
with binary rewards and drives models toward extreme overconfidence; across
decision benchmarks it wins accuracy while wrecking calibration. The naive
fix — supervised fine-tuning — calibrates better but gains less accuracy.
No single-stage recipe in common use delivers both.

It had not been solved in this setting because the two fixes live in
different stages: proper-scoring rewards shape \emph{distributions} during
training, while temperature scaling reshapes \emph{confidence} after it, and
prior work evaluates one or the other rather than composing them on a fixed
decision benchmark with distributional supervision. Our approach is the
composition: (i)~post-train a prefill-only decision model with
cross-entropy against soft teacher-averaged targets plus Brier and RPS
calibration terms, then (ii)~fit a single temperature on disjoint data.
On 400 held-out business-decision cases the composed model reaches 0.800
decision accuracy with ECE 0.031, against 0.665/0.091 for its base, 0.767
with ECE 0.214 for an accuracy-first specialist, and 0.532 with ECE 0.085
for a calibration-first baseline.

\begin{itemize}
\item We show proper-loss post-training on soft decision targets improves
accuracy, proper scores, and selective prediction together (Section~5).
\item We identify an \emph{underconfidence effect}: distributional
supervision spreads mass and depresses argmax confidence below argmax
accuracy, which ECE punishes symmetrically (Section~6).
\item We show a single honestly-fit temperature resolves the effect with
accuracy unchanged, yielding a model that dominates both the
accuracy-first and calibration-first baselines on both axes (Section~5).
\end{itemize}

\section{Background / Related Work}

Two methodological commitments separate this work from its neighbours, and
everything else is credit. First, \emph{confidence is the output
distribution, not a second channel.} The RLCR recipe~\cite{damani2025rlcr}
proves that correctness plus a bounded proper rule incentivises both
accuracy and calibration, but instantiates confidence as verbalised text
produced alongside the answer; we instantiate it as the decision
distribution itself, which is what a single-pass model returns and what
downstream thresholds consume. We follow RLCR's core theoretical
constraint — Brier, not log loss, as the calibration term — while
discarding the verbalisation machinery. Second, \emph{supervision is
distributional.} Where RLVR-style work rewards discrete hits and
SFT-style work distills hard labels, we train cross-entropy against
teacher-averaged soft targets, so the Brier and RPS terms refine an
already-distributional objective rather than fighting a discretised one.
The calibration-aware RL diagnosis of~\cite{yaldiz2026calibration} —
decision tokens inherit overconfidence with no calibrated paths to
reinforce — motivates our reasoning-free, single-pass architecture, in
which there are no reasoning traces to inherit from. TypeSafe's
RLCD program~\cite{typesafe2026rlcd} states the industrial version of our
objective; it publishes no recipe, curves, or training details, so it is a
goal statement we operationalise rather than prior art we build on. We
build directly on the open Kev post-training stack (causal LM, LoRA,
pointer readout) and evaluate inside a fixed typed-decision harness.
\section{Method}

The model outputs, for each typed question about a state, a full calibrated
distribution in one forward pass — no sampling, no reasoning trace. The key
idea is a two-stage composition: proper-scoring post-training shapes
\emph{distributions} (what mass goes where), and a single honestly-fit
temperature shapes \emph{confidence} (how peaked the reported distribution
is). Accuracy is invariant to the second stage, so the stages do not fight.

Concretely, consider Q5 of Figure~\ref{fig:running}, the \texttt{urgency}
score question with levels $\{0,1,2,3\}$ and gold
$\{2{:}0.50, 1{:}0.37, 3{:}0.10, 0{:}0.03\}$. A hard-label trainer sees only
``level 2'' and pushes all mass there; rewarded by 0/1 accuracy, the model
learns to report $\{2{:}0.99\}$, which is accurate and useless as a
probability. Our trainer instead minimises cross-entropy against the soft
gold vector, so the model is penalised for starving level~1 of its 0.37; the
Brier term additionally penalises squared deviation from the realised
outcome, and the RPS term penalises mass placed far from the realised level
along the ordinal scale. The measured output,
$\{2{:}0.56, 1{:}0.27, 3{:}0.14, 0{:}0.03\}$, tracks gold mass nearly
exactly — including its spread. That spread is the point of the first stage
and, as Section~6 shows, the entire content of the second: argmax accuracy
is high (level~2 correct) while argmax confidence (0.56) understates it, so
temperature $T<1$ sharpens the reported distribution onto the diagonal.

Formally, a case is a state $s$ (JSON) and questions $q$ each with $k_q$
options. The model is a causal LM run prefill-only with a pointer head:
logits $z \in \mathbb{R}^{k_q}$ at the option-boundary tokens, giving
$p = \mathrm{softmax}(z)$. Training minimises, per decision,
$\mathcal{L} = \mathrm{CE}(p, g) + 0.5\cdot\mathrm{Brier}(p, y) +
0.5\cdot\mathrm{RPS}(p, y)$ for score questions (Brier only for
choice/noul), where $g$ is the soft gold distribution and $y$ the discrete
label, starting from a released checkpoint (delta mode: LoRA rank 16 plus
pointer head). Inference reports $\mathrm{softmax}(z/T)$ with $T$ fit by NLL
on disjoint data ($T^\star = 0.35$ here). Design alternatives (loss-weight
grid, full-weight vs.\ LoRA, per-primitive temperatures) are future work,
not inline comparisons: exactly one configuration is trained and reported.

\section{Experimental Setup}

Datasets and splits: four business-decision workflows (agent-trace
observability, customer service, invoice processing, security incidents);
1,200 training cases (300 per workflow, 6,000 decisions) and 400 held-out
test cases (100 per workflow, 2,000 decisions, 1,945 scoring after context
rejects). Gold distributions average teacher judgments (5,977/6,000
non-degenerate; mean entropy 0.75 nats); rare labels ($<15$ train
occurrences, e.g.\ \texttt{action=stop}) bound tail-calibration claims.
Task and metrics: per-decision accuracy, ECE (15 bins), ECE after
temperature scaling, Brier, NLL, KL(gold$\|$pred), AURC, coverage at fixed
confidence, score MAE and RPS. Baselines and tuning: unmodified kev-4b base
(iora zero-shot, untuned — it is the ablation of our delta, not a
competitor); train-frequency prior (0.484) and uniform (0.318) reference
levels; an accuracy-first specialist fine-tuned on the same train split
(0.767 accuracy, ECE 0.214); a calibration-first NLI baseline (0.532,
0.085). Hyperparameters: lr 2e-5, 2 epochs, batch 1 $\times$ 8
accumulation, gradient checkpointing (required for 4B LoRA in 24~GB),
single fixed configuration — no search, so nothing to report. Compute: one
RTX 3090, $\approx$2~h, $\approx$\$0.30. Seeds and runs: every number in
this paper is a \emph{single run}; no variance is reported because only one
2-hour run was executed, and the temperature fit uses one fixed split. The
temperature-selection split (train) is disjoint from every number reported
on test.

\section{Results}

Three claims, one subsection each.

\paragraph{Proper-loss post-training improves accuracy, proper scores, and
selective prediction together (Table~\ref{tab:main}).} Decision accuracy
rises 0.665 $\to$ 0.800; NLL 0.819 $\to$ 0.576; Brier 0.468 $\to$ 0.327;
AURC 0.195 $\to$ 0.071; score MAE 0.603 $\to$ 0.390; RPS 0.117 $\to$ 0.066.
Coverage at confidence $\ge 0.9$ more than doubles (0.059 $\to$ 0.130) with
accuracy 1.000 inside the band. Gains hold in every workflow
(agent-trace 0.568 $\to$ 0.754; customer-service 0.706 $\to$ 0.822; invoice
0.692 $\to$ 0.840; security 0.700 $\to$ 0.786) and every primitive,
ruling out single-slice luck.

\paragraph{ECE regresses overall, driven entirely by score questions.}
Overall ECE moves 0.091 $\to$ 0.162 against the accuracy gains — the same
shape as the accuracy-first specialist (0.767 accuracy, 0.214 ECE).
Decomposed, choice ECE moves 0.104 $\to$ 0.149, noul is flat
0.111 $\to$ 0.107, and score collapses 0.074 $\to$ 0.212. Reporting accuracy
alone would hide this; reporting ECE alone would hide the accuracy, Brier,
and selection gains. This negative result is a finding, not a footnote,
and Section~6 explains it.

\paragraph{An honestly-fit temperature resolves the regression with
accuracy unchanged.} $T^\star = 0.35$ minimises NLL on train-split logits
(disjoint from test) and is applied once to test: ECE 0.162 $\to$ 0.031
overall (0.083 choice, 0.048 noul, 0.026 score), NLL 0.576 $\to$ 0.464,
accuracy fixed at 0.800 by construction. KL rises 0.079 $\to$ 0.469: the
temperature knob makes the sharpness–fidelity tradeoff explicit rather than
eliminating it. The composed model (0.800 accuracy, 0.031 ECE) dominates
the accuracy-first specialist (0.767, 0.214) and the calibration-first
baseline (0.532, 0.085) on both axes simultaneously.
\section{Analysis and Limitations}

The results carry the logic that the claim holds; here is what they mean
and where they stop. Post-training against soft targets plus proper
scores, followed by honest temperature scaling, yields a small single-pass
model that is simultaneously more accurate and better calibrated than
either single-axis baseline. The mechanism is the composition itself:
proper losses shape \emph{where} mass goes (hence the KL and Brier wins),
temperature shapes \emph{how peaked} the report is (hence the ECE win),
and because temperature preserves argmax the two stages cannot fight. The
reliability curves (Figure~\ref{fig:rel}) show it: the unscaled delta sits
below the diagonal (underconfident at every confidence level), and
$T^\star$ rotates it onto the diagonal.

The bounds are mechanistic, not tabular. First, the method \emph{requires}
distributional supervision to produce the underconfidence that temperature
then corrects: trained on hard labels, the same pipeline would inherit the
standard overconfidence pathology and temperature $>1$ would be doing
different work, untested here. Second, a single global temperature assumes
miscalibration has one direction; choice, noul, and score lean the same way
here (all improve under $T<1$), but a deployment mixing over- and
underconfident question families would need per-primitive temperatures,
also untested. Third, the temperature-selection split is the training
distribution: under genuine distribution shift the fitted $T^\star$ is the
first thing to distrust, and nothing here measures that decay. Fourth, tail
calibration is unmeasured by construction — rare labels have single-digit
training counts, so all ECE claims concern head behavior. Finally, the
temperature-search hypothesis was formed on test-set oracle traces before
being validated by the honest train-fit split; the direction was
post-hoc, the validation was not, and the paper reports both in that
order. All headline numbers are single runs; variability is unreported for
the stated compute reason.

\section{Conclusion}

Small decision models need not choose between accuracy and calibration if
the two are optimised in the stages that control them: proper-scoring
post-training for distributions, honestly-fit temperature for confidence.
The composed 4B model reaches 0.800 accuracy at 0.031 ECE where its base
stood at 0.665/0.091 — with the mechanism (soft-target underconfidence
corrected by sharpening) made explicit rather than averaged away. Framed
as the method's own limitation: the recipe trusts its temperature-fit
distribution, and its next test should be shift, not scale.
